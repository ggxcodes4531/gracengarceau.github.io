Inverters for Grid-Connected Photovoltaic Systems: Getting Power from Solar Panels

In the simplest terms, an inverter takes direct current (DC) from solar panels (photovoltaic systems) and converts it into alternating current (AC) that is compatible with the electrical grid.

Why is this conversion needed? Solar panels generate DC, which is easy to store in power banks and provides steady power to devices like phones. DC is like a heavy wheel of Parmesan cheese: it provides a consistent amount of cheese until it runs out. The electrical grid, however, mostly uses AC because its voltage is easy to change and it can travel long distances.

The inverter uses electronic switches to convert DC into AC. Imagine a child at swim class kicking their legs quickly: as one leg goes down, the other moves up. The inverter similarly switches the direction of the current. H-bridges using insulated gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs) are commonly used because they can handle high voltages and currents.

The inverter continuously uses a maximum power point tracking (MPPT) algorithm to find the voltage and current that draw as much power as possible from the solar panels. If the grid goes down, anti-islanding protection disconnects the inverter from it. The inverter is also like a chameleon, adapting to its surroundings while protecting itself.

Direct Ink Writing (DIW): Conductive Inks for Soft Electronics

Direct ink writing (DIW) enables rapid, customized prototyping on softer materials, especially for electronics. Because the required ink can be difficult to find, scientists worked collaboratively to develop an alternative dilute, silver-flake-based ink.

The reported formulation describes “10,000 g/min PS- and PU-based formulas at 40% u/v silver loading.” Reduced spacing between electrodes is expected to create electric fields comparable to those in commercial systems operating at lower voltages. In other words, the system can drive current at a lower rate, reducing negative side effects such as electrochemical reactions.

How conductivity develops

  • Silver flakes or carbon black can be used to develop conductivity, applying percolation theory to an otherwise insulating polymer.
  • As the volume fraction of conductive material increases, clusters of conductive particles form. After the percolation threshold is reached, the particles connect across clusters, producing an S-shaped conductivity curve.
  • Conductivity can be correlated with the viscosity of the ink.
